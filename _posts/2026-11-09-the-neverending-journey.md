# The neverending journey
## A Departure I Wish I could Forget
I had one more beer at the floating bar in Vava'u Tonga. One last one for the road. Said goodbye to the sailors around me and hopped into my beaten down, Chinese branded 2 person dinghy, the air floor sadly deflated. I fired up my much newer, but of similar origin, 2.5HP two stroke, and pootled across the bay to my home for the last 3 years, Lordship, a 38 foot Westerly Sealord. I was in no rush. I like to start all my off shore passages so that I'm heading away from land around sunset. I have a lot of nervous energy, and I know I will stay up all night keeping a proper watch. My theory is that when I'm napping in the middle of the next day any traffic close to shore, with the appropriate watch, should see me. It hasn't failed me so far. 

Although I had done quite a few solo offshore passages over the last couple of years, this one was different, it was to be my last one. After leaving from Portugal in December 2021, 3 years sailing almost half way around the world had taken its toll on my bank balance, why didn't anyone tell me this sailing malarky was expensive? I started 2024 on a high, completing a solo pacific crossing. I continued mostly solo across the Pacific and by the time I left French Polynesia I had decided to head to NZ at the end of the season to sell the boat, and return to the drudgery of land based life.

The overnight passage had little of note, the wind was brisk, but on the beam, so it wasn't too uncomfortable. As the sun rose up, I was standing on the side deck, cup of tea in hand, wondering at glorious nature of my headsail, full and firm in the breeze, pushing me along the ocean waterway. Unfortunately I followed the sweet lines all the way to head of the sail, where I saw a few strands of wire fraying out of the top of the forestay at the tang. 

I don't know if the materials were faulty, the swagging was faulty, the rig wasn't tuned correctly, or there is a skill issue with me, but I'd experienced the same failure in my aft lowers in 2023 in the Carribean while on passage form Martinique to Cuba. The rig had been replaced in 2019, and now 3 of the cables had failed in the same way. When I discovered the aft lower fault, there was some part of me that considered continuing the journey, how important were aft lowers anyway? Very important according to the rigger I emailed at the time. Given my intended destination was Cuba, where I'd have no chance of repairing them, I decided to divert to the Dominican Republic. This is to say, I was aware of the serious nature of frayed cables on a forestay, and had no hesitation rolling away the headsail, reefing the main down to 3rd reef, and motor sailing for the nearest land mass.

I sloped into the anchorage in the north of the Ha'apia group towards the end of Friday the 27th. The dates will, sadly, become relevant to this story. I dropped my anchor and retired for the evening, without thinking too much about the problem. It's always better to face problems well rested if possible. The next morning, cruising friends, who hadn't expected to see me again, possibly ever, came over to see why I was in the bay and not on my way to NZ. In short order I gave them the unenviable task of winching me up the mast to assess the damage. I counted 10 of the 18 strands of my 8mm forestay cable had broken off at the tang. We jury rigged a temporary stay using a length of dyneema from the anchor roller to the top of the mast. It was clear that I couldn't sail to NZ with the rig in that condition and I'd have to check back into Tonga and get the forestay replaced.
## The Nightmare Begins
On Monday I borrowed a folding bike and cycled 13km from the anchorage to the customs office located in the main town of Pangai. I presented my, somewhat perspiring, self to the customs officer and explained the situation. As she examined my exit papers and my passport exit stamp, her brow furrowed in consternation. Eventually she figured it out, her red pen came out, she'd caught me red handed. The red pen crossed out my exit stamp, CANCELLED, the same treatment was given to my clearance papers. 
> You've over stayed your visa!
For indeed my visa did expire on the 28th of September, two days after I cleared out of the country and left, but as the customs officers don't see fit to work at the weekend, it was now September the 30th.

I was somewhat taken aback, but not completely, I'd checked in and out of 20+ countries, so I knew that sometimes customs officers can get a little carried away with the authority they perceive to be invested in them by the highest of powers, God. I tried politely explaining to her that even though today was the 30th, and I was still in Tonga, I was here because I had a life threatening emergency at sea and had to return for safety. It was to no avail. She informed me that I must now go back to Vava'u where I would have to pay a fine of $1,000TOP ($450USD). Then I would be free to repair my boat and check out again. I will confess I was quite indignant at this turn of events, however I remained calm and polite. I calmly and politely took my passport out of her hand when she told me that she would keep it until I was ready to depart for Vava'u. 

As soon as I left the office I contacted the British foreign office. 
> Put me through to the Embassy old chap, I'm in a spot of bother in Tonga.
I was put through to the New Zealand consulate. The proceeding conversation left me even more indignant. I expect the Tongan custom officials not to be predisposed to helping a British sailor out, but to get the same treatment from one's own government, really heaps ignominy upon indignity. The line from the customer support agent at Her Majesties Government was that the British Embassy would in no way be willing to render any assistance in this matter, they would be happy to email me a list of lawyers in Tonga if that was of any consolation. It wasn't.

Once I made it back to the anchorage and debriefed my friends, a war counsel was formed over sun downers. Who had contacts in the Tongan Government, was it worth contacting the UK embassy again? In the end a connection in the Tongan Tourism department was emailed. The Tongan High Commission in the UK was emailed. A budding yachting agent in Vava'u was enlisted to visit the customs team there to plead my case. Tongan Customs at the HQ were phoned numerous times over the following days. All of it was for naught. 

At the same time I contacted the only boat yard in Vava'u, epistemically named, The Boat Yard. They informed me they had no qualified rigger, no swagging machine, or parts. They offered to help me if I wanted to replace the forestay on my own and was willing to wait 6 - 12 weeks for a replacement, that I'd have to measure myself, to be delivered from New Zealand. Making the upwind journey back to Vava'u was becoming increasingly unappealing, there was no chance I'd be able to sail upwind 100NM, and like any true cruiser, my engine worked, but only grudgingly after a few kicks and a stern warning. I wouldn't want to bet my life on it running 20 hours without a hiccup. Conversely the rigging company in Fiji confirmed that they had the materials, the equipment and would be able to fix the boat and make it seaworthy in short order so that I could sail down to New Zealand before cyclone season begun.

By Wednesday, I had shifted my mindset, I wasn't staying in Tonga. I was going to sail cautiously down wind to Fiji, repair my boat and finish this journey. As a naive and honest chap, I kept all authorities in the loop of all my plans. Tongan officials were informed that I wished to continue my journey to Fiji where I could repair my boat, and asked if they could return my exit papers, or create new ones. This request was summarily denied. I expected no less. I followed Fiji's pre-arrival requirements and informed them precisely of the situation I was in. On the Friday, I gave up trying to reason with Tonga and set sail. I had received no response to my pre-arrival notification to Fiji.

## Fiji, The Way The World Should Be
It took me 5 and a half days to sail the 500NM from Tonga to Fiji. Reefed main, pocket hanker-chief of headsail. We had following seas and 15 - 20kts of wind. It was extremely pleasant sailing. I arrived into Port Denarau in Fiji on Wednesday the 9th of October at 8am. Many of you will have checked into foreign countries so will not be surprised to hear how disorganised the officials were, so I'll skip that and focus on the more entertaining aspects of it. 

My lawyer would advise me against this next section, which is why I didn't seek their advice before writing this article. Having discussed with friends and colleagues what I could do to ensure a smooth entry into Fiji, I was advised to leave out a carton, that is to say 10 packs, of cigarettes in a very obvious place. When asked, for surely I would be asked about them, to say that I was quitting smoking so I had gotten them out to give them away to friends in Fiji. I could not believe that such a preposterous charade would ever be engaged with by the fine and upstanding constabulary of Fiji Revenue and Customs, but as usual, I was wrong. It was the first order of business and the officer promptly offered to take them off my hands.

After this we got down to brass tacks, where were my exit papers? He had not read the pre-arrival notifications, in fact, on me asking him that, he demanded that I (without internet) prove to him that I had indeed sent the pre-arrival notification. As this was not my first rodeo I had already downloaded copies to show him. He read it, and asked me to go through and explain why I didn't have clearance papers, which I did, I pointed out the damage to my mast. Having completed a cursory inspection of the boat and pronouncing it bio secure and not containing any contraband, we went back to shore. At shore I was made to give my story again to immigration. At which point the customs officer told me he'd need to write a report and that he'd call me in the afternoon.

That afternoon they summoned me back to the office. The customs officer told me that I needed to pay the biosecurity fee, an unexplained (but unquestioned) overtime fee for customs, and that it needed to be paid in cash and it needed to be paid within the hour, they were shutting early for Fiji's independence day the following day. I was elated, I ran around Denarau like a headless chicken. Of course my cards didn't work for withdrawing money at any of the ATMs near by. In the end I had to go to a western union to get money. I came back with 10 minutes to spare, handed over the money, got my immigration stamp, got my biosecurity certificate. The customs officer puffed up his chest and said:
> Mr. Brown, I will not be clearing your vessel into Fiji, I have referred you for investigation.
I didn't even demand my cigarettes back.

## Interview under Caution
It is still so surreal to recall that interview. The investigating officer told me that I was being interviewed under caution and that I had the right to have an attorney present, would I like one? I said no. 

When watching the police stitch up some hopeless ignorant sop in a TV drama, and they agree to be interviewed without a lawyer, I would smugly think, I would never let the police interview me without a lawyer, I'm way too smart for that. Well I did. I was still thinking, this is a self important pompous over reaction, and they're going to demand I grovel to them (which I'd already done repeatedly) and then they're going to give me an administrative fine, like the $1,000TOP in Tonga, which I didn't feel I should pay, but I would pay as long as I could get my boat fixed and get down to New Zealand. So I agreed to be interviewed under caution without a lawyer present.

The interview was not at all combative. The officer simply asked me to repeat the same story I'd given before as he typed out my answers. He asked some background about how long I'd owned the boat, where I'd been and what happened on the voyage that lead to me putting back into to Tonga. He asked why I didn't go back to Vava'u, and I gave the safety based answer, which I'd given to Tongan officials and in the pre-arrival notification. I essentially told my true account of the situation for the 4th or 5th time that week.

This is where it gets interesting. After the interview was done, I had checked it and signed it, I asked the investigating officer 'off the record': what would happen next? He said it was up to his bosses to decide if they would charge me. I asked if that happened, what would be the consequences. He told me that similar cases lead to fines of $12,000FJD ($5,500USD). The original customs officer, sat at his desk next to us, couldn't believe it, he asked the investigating officer:
> Sēchi*, did you say $12,000?
The investigating officer angrily shushed him. I told the investigating officer that I hoped his bosses could see that in these circumstances I was simply trying to do what was safest for my boat, and that I hoped they'd decide not to charge me.

* Get out of here! Strong negation/disbelief. (find source)

I then had to wait for the rest of the day to hear back from the investigating officer. The day passed in a state of heightened anxiety as you may well imagine. If the $1,000TOP was stretching my meagre funds, $12,000FJD was an unthinkable amount of money, and one that I wouldn't be able to raise without help from family.

At 5:00pm I received a Whatsapp from the investigating officer. 
> Please come to the Denarau Customs office in 30 minutes.

I arrived at 5:30pm, the investigating officer and his partner arrived around 45 minutes later, as the sun was setting. He told me that his bosses had decided to charge me with arriving to Fiji without proper customs clearance. This was done standing in the carpark in front of his government SUV, as the office was closed. He handed me a letter with the charge paperwork and asked me to sign it on the bonnet. I was still somewhat in a state of disbelief at the level of wanton callousness behind cheery smiles that would become a theme of my time in Fiji. I took one last roll of the dice, I apologised again, and said something to the effect:
> I only want to fix my mast and leave as soon as possible, how do I facilitate that?

His response was simple:
> If you give me $12,000FJD right now in cash, then you will be able to do that.

I was astounded, $12,000FJD in cash? It was mind boggling. I told him that there was no physical way for me to even obtain $12,000FJD in cash today. He smiled and said:
> That's OK sir, I have a card machine in my car that you can use.
## Fighting the charges
I had 14 days to answer the charge. The way that Fiji Customs laws are written, the offence I committed, not presenting last port clearance papers when requested, fell under the same set of offences for drug smuggling, arms & human trafficking, and the punishment scale was the same for all of them, up to 12 years in prison and/or a fine no larger than $100,000FJD. This fact was used on numerous occasions by the investigating officer to imply that the $12,000FJD fine was, in their view, a lenient penalty for what I had done. My options at this point were to admit in writing that I was guilty of the offence and to ask for the Comptroller of customs (CEO of Fiji Revenue & Customs) to compound the offence, which means to assess my case and mitigating circumstances, and to assign a fine amount. After that was paid, the offence would be removed and I would be allowed into the country. The other option was to contest the charge in Fiji's legal courts. 

The problem with the first option, the negotiated settlement, was that there was no negotiation. Somehow the Comptroller had already set the fine amount before even receiving my request for compounding. It was also stated that once I plead guilty and asked for compounding, I gave up any legal right to challenge the outcome. Further, Fiji Revenue and Customs refused to put in writing what the fine amount would be, the officer denied saying the fine amount _was_ $12,000FJD, merely that he was informing me that previous cases had _been_ compounded at $12,000FJD. I was stuck in between a rock and a hard place and left with the unenviable task of trusting a government who had so far shown zero evidence of credibility or trustworthiness.

I spent a lot of time on my boat thinking about what I could do. The advice from many experienced sailors was to GTFO as soon as possible without further engagement with rogue nation states, which I was in full agreement with, but was unable to safely do with my broken forestay. I came extremely close in those two weeks to signing the paper work and borrowing the money.

On the day before decision day, I received a call from a local lawyer, who had been advised of my plight. She was what might be described in antiquated parlance, a real pitbull of a lawyer. Her advice summed up to:
> They're corrupt. That's corruption. We're going to get them on that. Don't give them a cent.
She also offered to do the case pro bono, so I thought why not, she isn't wrong, let's win in court.
## Two Years Later 
There were many amusing and bizarre occurrences over the following two years during which I settled in Suva, the capital of Fiji. I occupied my time by starting a Brazilian Jiu-jitsu and Mixed Martial Arts school, and by building a 11 foot plywood stitch and glue dinghy.

When I reflect on those two years, there is one thing that I learnt and re-learnt time and time again. There are two concepts, Justice and The Law, and probably throughout the world, but for sure in Fiji, those two are very often complete strangers to each other.

I will relate only one illuminating aspect of the case. I will forego a detailed description of the thrill of standing in the dock at Nadi Magistrates Court, charged with a criminal offence and released on bail, although I did enjoy that quite a lot.

After about a year of painfully slow progress in the courts, I was very keen to call it all quits and negotiate a settlement. I met with Fiji Revenue and Customs with my lawyer. During that meeting we made several generous offers to them to settle, but all were rejected. When we asked why they agreed to the meeting if they were not willing to settle, they said they would be willing to settle at $10,000FJD. My lawyer asked the investigating officer why they were acting so unreasonable. It was a way to draw out information on their case. The investigating officer eagerly explained that in their eyes I had committed a grave and aggravated offence when I had disobeyed the direction from Tongan customs officers. Not only that, but they had evidence that I could have repaired my boat in Tonga, and I was lying about the necessity of my travel to Fiji. This was the second astonishing statement the officer had made to me, given we had already been through discovery and there was no evidence of any investigative work in discovery. My lawyer asked him how he would present any of these aggravating factors given none of his so called investigative work was in discovery. The man brazenly told us that didn't matter, he would still present it.

So there was some perverse and petty pleasure during his time on the witness stand when he started telling the court how he had communicated with the Tongan officials and they had told him...
> Objection: Hearsay, none of this is in evidence.

> Sustained.

Even more pleasing when he started arguing with the Magistrate about how he should be allowed to present the hearsay, the magistrate was not happy with that. I do believe the fellow was in danger of being found in contempt of court.

In the end I was acquitted. The case that we presented, and the judgement, rested mostly upon the wording of Fijian Customs law which states:
> the master of the vessel must present, when requested, the outward clearance from the last port, _if any_, to the customs officer.
The phrase, if any, my lawyer argued, indicated that the law allowed for certain circumstances when customs clearance my not be available lawfully. The magistrate agreed and acquitted me. The nature of damage to my vessel, the necessity of me to get it repaired, were contributing factors. Which means the judgement doesn't allow you to arrive in Fiji without clearance papers under normal circumstances.

After I was acquitted, I felt a surge of relief. It was, however, tempered with a practical understanding that while my position had improved, I was not 'in the clear'. Fijian customs appear from the outside to be corrupt. They have their own way of working, and my boat was still here without proper paperwork. I needed some cooperation from Customs to allow me to either check the boat into the country, or get the boat ready for off shore sailing (it has been held unused for 23 months), and leave without checking in. At the time of writing this article Customs are not being cooperative and have mounted various bureaucratic obstacles to getting the boat ready to depart, or for checking the boat into the country. I was not surprised by this and am doing all I possibly can under the restrictions they've imposed to prepare the boat to leave.

I will end the article by saying, even though I make light of it in this retelling, there were times when my mental state severely deteriorated. It requires a great amount of mental energy to go about your day as a criminal on bail, even if you know that you are innocent. There were no legitimate ways out of the situation. I could pay what I would say was effectively a bribe and contribute to corruption, I could actually commit a criminal act by leaving without paperwork when my boat and I were safe and repaired, or I could abandon my boat and leave the country by air.
