# The neverending journey
## A Departure I Wish I could Forget
I had one more beer at the floating bar in Vava'u Tonga. One last one for the road. Said goodbye to the sailors around me and hopped into my beaten down, Chinese branded 2 person dinghy, the air floor sadly deflated. I fired up my much newer, but of similar origin, 2.5HP two stroke, and pootled across the bay to my home for the last 3 years, Lordship, a 38 foot Westerly Sealord. I was in no rush. I like to start all my off shore passages so that I'm heading away from land around sunset. I have a lot of nervous energy, and I know I will stay up all night keeping a proper watch. My theory is that when I'm napping in the middle of the next day any traffic close to shore, with the appropriate watch, should see me. It hasn't failed me so far. 

Although I had done quite a few solo offshore passages over the last couple of years, this one was different, it was to be my last one. After leaving from Portugal in December 2021, 3 years sailing almost half way around the world had taken its toll on my bank balance, why didn't anyone tell me this sailing malarky was expensive? I started 2024 on a high, completing a solo pacific crossing. I continued mostly solo across the Pacific and by the time I left French Polynesia I had decided to head to NZ at the end of the season to sell the boat, and return to the drudgery of land based life.

The overnight passage had little of note, the wind was brisk, but on the beam, so it wasn't too uncomfortable. As the sun rose up, I was standing on the side deck, cup of tea in hand, wondering at glorious nature of my headsail, full and firm in the breeze, pushing me along the ocean waterway. Unfortunately I followed the sweet lines all the way to head of the sail, where I saw a few strands of wire fraying out of the top of the forestay at the tang. 

I don't know if the materials were faulty, the swagging was faulty, the rig wasn't tuned correctly, or there is a skill issue with me, but I'd experienced the same failure in my aft lowers in 2023 in the Carribean while on passage form Martinique to Cuba. The rig had been replaced in 2019, and now 3 of the cables had failed in the same way. When I discovered the aft lower fault, there was some part of me that considered continuing the journey, how important were aft lowers anyway? Very important according to the rigger I emailed at the time. Given my intended destination was Cuba, where I'd have no chance of repairing them, I decided to divert to the Dominican Republic. This is to say, I was aware of the serious nature of frayed cables on a forestay, and had no hesitation rolling away the headsail, reefing the main down to 3rd reef, and motor sailing for the nearest land mass.

I sloped into the anchorage in the north of the Ha'apia group towards the end of Friday the 27th. The dates will, sadly, become relevant to this story. I dropped my anchor and retired for the evening, without thinking too much about the problem. It's always better to face problems well rested if possible. The next morning, cruising friends, who hadn't expected to see me again, possibly ever, came over to see why I was in the bay and not on my way to NZ. In short order I gave them the unenviable task of winching me up the mast to assess the damage. I counted 10 of the 18 strands of my 8mm forestay cable had broken off at the tang. We jury rigged a temporary stay using a length of dyneema from the anchor roller to the top of the mast. It was clear that I couldn't sail to NZ with the rig in that condition and I'd have to check back into Tonga and get the forestay replaced.
## The Nightmare Begins
On Monday I borrowed a folding bike and cycled 13km from the anchorage to the customs office located in the main town of Pangai. I presented my, somewhat perspiring, self to the customs officer and explained the situation. As she examined my exit papers and my passport exit stamp, her brow furrowed in consternation. Eventually she figured it out, her red pen came out, she'd caught me red handed. The red pen crossed out my exit stamp, CANCELLED, the same treatment was given to my clearance papers. 
> You've over stayed your visa!

For indeed my visa did expire on the 28th of September, two days after I cleared out of the country and left, but as the customs officers don't see fit to work at the weekend, it was now September the 30th.

I was somewhat taken aback, but not completely, I'd checked in and out of 20+ countries, so I knew that sometimes customs officers can get a little carried away with the authority they perceive to be invested in them by the highest of powers, God. I tried politely explaining to her that even though today was the 30th, and I was still in Tonga, I was here because I had a life threatening emergency at sea and had to return for safety. It was to no avail. She informed me that I must now go back to Vava'u where I would have to pay a fine of $1,000TOP ($450USD). Then I would be free to repair my boat and check out again. I will confess I was quite indignant at this turn of events, however I remained calm and polite. I calmly and politely took my passport out of her hand when she told me that she would keep it until I was ready to depart for Vava'u. 

As soon as I left the office I contacted the British foreign office. 
> Put me through to the Embassy, old chap, I'm in a spot of bother in Tonga.

I was put through to the New Zealand consulate. I expect the Tongan custom officials not to be predisposed to helping a British sailor out, but to get the same treatment from one's own government, really heaps ignominy upon indignity. The line from the customer support agent at Her Majesties Government was that the British Embassy would in no way be willing to render any assistance in this matter, they would be happy to email me a list of lawyers in Tonga if that was of any consolation.

Once I made it back to the anchorage and debriefed my friends, a war counsel was formed over sun downers. Who had contacts in the Tongan Government, was it worth contacting the UK embassy again? Lots of Tongan officials were contacted, the UK embassy was not contacted as they're about as useful as a chocolate teapot. Tongan Customs were phoned numerous times over the following days. All of it was for naught. 

I contacted the only boat yard in Vava'u. They had no qualified rigger, no swagging machine, or parts. I would have to replace the forestay myself at their yard, measuring and ordering it from New Zealand with a 6 week minimum wait for delivery. Making the upwind journey back to Vava'u was becoming increasingly unappealing, there was no chance I'd be able to sail upwind 100NM, and like any true cruiser, my engine worked, but only grudgingly after a few kicks and a stern warning. I wouldn't want to bet my life on it running 20 hours without a hiccup. A rigging company in Fiji confirmed that they would be able to fix the boat and make it seaworthy in short order so that I could sail down to New Zealand before cyclone season began.

By Wednesday I had shifted my mindset, I wasn't staying in Tonga, I was going to sail cautiously down wind to Fiji. A naive and honest chap, I kept all authorities in the loop. Tongan officials were informed that I wished to continue my journey to Fiji and I asked if they could return my exit papers. This request was summarily denied. I followed Fiji's pre-arrival requirements and informed them precisely of the situation I was in. On the Friday, I gave up trying to reason with Tonga and set sail. I had received no response to my pre-arrival notification to Fiji.

## Fiji, The Way The World Should Be
It took me 5 and a half days to sail the 500NM from Tonga to Fiji. Reefed main, pocket hanker-chief of headsail. We had following seas and 15 - 20kts of wind. It was extremely pleasant sailing. I arrived into Port Denarau in Fiji on Wednesday the 9th of October at 8am.

My lawyer would advise me against this next section, which is why I didn't seek their advice before writing this article. Having discussed with friends and colleagues what I could do to ensure a smooth entry into Fiji, I was advised to leave out a carton, that is to say 10 packs, of cigarettes in a very obvious place. When asked, for surely I would be asked about them, to say that I was quitting smoking so I had gotten them out to give them away to friends in Fiji. 

Once the customs officials boarded my ship, the officer inquired where were my exit papers? He had not read the pre-arrival notifications, I expected nothing less. I explained the situation. Once they completed a cursory inspection of the boat and pronounced it biosecure and not containing any contraband, we went back to shore, where I was made to give my story again to immigration. The customs officer said he would need to write a report and so dismissed me.

That afternoon they summoned me back to the office. I needed to pay the biosecurity fee, an unexplained (but unquestioned) overtime fee for customs, in cash and within the hour - they were shutting early for Fiji's independence day the following day. I was elated, it looked like the ordeal was finally over, I ran around Denarau like a headless chicken. Of course my cards didn't work for withdrawing money at any of the ATMs near by. In the end I had to go to a Western Union. I came back with 10 minutes to spare, handed over the money, got my immigration stamp, got my biosecurity certificate. The customs officer puffed up his chest and said:
> Mr. Brown, I will not be clearing your vessel into Fiji, I have referred you for investigation.

I didn't even demand my cigarettes back.

## Interview under Caution
It is still so surreal to recall that interview. The investigating officer told me that I was being interviewed under caution and that I had the right to have an attorney present, would I like one. I declined, as all hapless idiots do. 

The interview was not at all combative. The officer simply asked me to repeat the same story I'd given before as he typed out my answers. He asked some background about how long I'd owned the boat, where I'd been and what happened on the voyage from Tonga. He asked why I didn't go back to Vava'u, and I gave the safety based answer, which I'd given to Tongan officials and in the pre-arrival notification email. I essentially told my true account of the situation for the 4th or 5th time that week.

After the interview was done, I asked the investigating officer 'off the record': what would happen next? He said it was up to his bosses to decide if they would charge me and the fine would be $12,000FJD ($5,500USD). The original customs officer, sat at his desk next to us, couldn't believe it, he asked the investigating officer:
> Sēchi*, did you say $12,000?

The investigating officer angrily shushed him.
* Get out of here! Strong negation/disbelief. (find source)

I then had to wait for the rest of the day to hear back from Customs. If the $1,000TOP ($400USD) was stretching my meagre funds, $12,000FJD ($5,500USD) was an unthinkable amount of money.

At 5:00pm I received a Whatsapp from the investigating officer. 
> Please come to the Denarau Customs office in 30 minutes.

I arrived at 5:30pm, the investigating officer and his partner arrived around 45 minutes later, as the sun was setting. He told me that his bosses had decided to charge me with arriving to Fiji without proper customs clearance. This was done standing in the carpark in front of his government SUV, as the office closed at 5pm and he didn't feel like this business needed to happen in the office. He handed me a letter with the charge paperwork and asked me to sign it on the bonnet. I was still somewhat in a state of disbelief at the level of wanton callousness behind cheery smiles that would become a theme of my time in Fiji. I took one last roll of the dice, I apologised again, and said something to the effect:
> I only want to fix my mast and leave as soon as possible, how do I facilitate that?

His response was:
> If you give me $12,000FJD right now in cash Mr. Brown, then you will be able to do that.

I was astounded, $12,000FJD in cash? It was mind boggling. I told him that there was no physical way for me to even obtain $12,000FJD in cash today. He smiled and said:
> That's OK sir, I have a card machine in my car that you can use.

## Fighting the charges
I had 14 days to answer the charge. The offence I committed, not presenting last port clearance papers when requested, fell under the same set of offences for drug smuggling, arms & human trafficking, and the punishment scale was the same for all of them, up to 12 years in prison and/or a fine no larger than $100,000FJD. This fact was used on numerous occasions by the investigating officer to imply that the $12,000FJD fine was, in their view, a lenient penalty for what I had done. My options at this point were to admit I was guilty of the offence and to ask for the Comptroller of customs (CEO of Fiji Revenue & Customs) to compound the offence. Compounding means to assess my case, the mitigating circumstances, and to assign a fine amount. After that was paid, the charges would be dropped and I would be allowed into the country without any record of the charge. The other option was to contest the charge in Fiji's legal courts. 

The problem with the first option, the negotiated settlement, was that there was no negotiation. Somehow the Comptroller had already set the fine amount before even receiving my request for compounding. By compounding, I gave up any legal right to challenge the outcome. Further, Fiji Revenue and Customs refused to put in writing what the fine amount would be, the officer denied saying the fine amount _was_ $12,000FJD, merely that he was informing me that previous cases had _been_ compounded at $12,000FJD. This kind of bureaucratic nonsense is common with FRCS. Essentially making up the details of rules and processes, saying one thing in person, but denying it in writing.

I spent a lot of time on my boat thinking about what I could do. The advice from many experienced sailors was to GTFO as soon as possible without further engagement with rogue nation states, which I was in full agreement with, but was unable to safely do with my broken forestay. I came extremely close in those two weeks to signing the paper work and borrowing the money.

On the last day I received a call from a local lawyer, who had been advised of my plight. She might be described in antiquated parlance as a real "pitbull" of a lawyer. Her advice summed up to:
> They're corrupt. Don't give them a cent.

She also offered to do the case pro bono, so I thought why not, she isn't wrong, let's win in court.
## Two Years Later 
Over the following two years I occupied my time in Suva by starting a martial arts school, and by building an 11 foot plywood dinghy. There is one thing that I learnt and re-learnt over those two years; Justice and The Law are very often complete strangers.

I will relate only one illuminating aspect of the case, foregoing a detailed description of the thrill of standing in the dock at Nadi Magistrates Court, charged with a criminal offence and being released on bail, although I enjoyed that quite a lot.

After about a year of painfully slow progress in the courts, I was very keen to call it all quits and negotiate a settlement, which was indeed my goal from the beginning. I met with Fiji Revenue and Customs with my lawyer. During that meeting we made several generous offers, but all were rejected. When we asked why they agreed to the meeting if they were not willing to settle, they said they would be willing to settle at $10,000FJD. If it seems to you on reading this account like they are a completely unserious organisation, that would be the correct reading. My lawyer asked the investigating officer why they were acting so unreasonable. It was a way to draw out information on their case. The investigating officer eagerly explained that in their eyes I had committed a grave and aggravated offence when I had disobeyed the direction from Tongan customs officers. Not only that, but they had evidence that I could have repaired my boat in Tonga, and I was lying about the necessity of my travel to Fiji. This officer, for the second time, left me astounded. We had already been through discovery and there was no evidence of any investigative work in discovery. My lawyer asked him how he would present any of these aggravating factors given none of his evidence was in discovery. The man brazenly told us that didn't matter, he would still present it.

During the hearing I derived some perverse and petty pleasure during his time on the witness stand when he started telling the court how he had communicated with the Tongan officials and they had told him that Mr. Brown...
> My lawyer: "Objection; Hearsay, none of this is in evidence."

> Magistrate: "Sustained."

Even more pleasing when he started arguing with the Magistrate about how he should be allowed to present the hearsay, the magistrate asked him in a stone cold tone "Are you questioning the authority of the court?". I do believe the fellow was in danger of being found in contempt of court.

I was acquitted. The case that we presented, and the judgement, rested mostly upon the wording of Fijian Customs law which states:
> the master of the vessel must present, when requested, the outward clearance from the last port, _if any_, to the customs officer.

The phrase, if any, my lawyer argued, indicated that the law allowed for certain circumstances when customs clearance my not be lawfully available. Both Fijian customs officers admitted on the stand that Fiji was a signatory to international maritime treaties (SOLAS & UNCLOS), and those treaties do allow for damaged vessels to seek assistance from member states. The magistrate ruled that FRCS had failed to prove it's case against me, that Fijian law does allow for certain circumstances when papers may not be available and that mine was one of them.

My joyous exhalation was tempered with a practical understanding that while my position had improved, I was not 'in the clear'. My boat was still in Fiji without proper paperwork, and I either needed to be cleared in to Fiji, or cleared out and given clearance papers to move on. The power essentially still lay with Customs, and losing in court didn't make them pre-disposed to resolving the issue. My boat has been in the water for over 3 years, and was now suffering severe osmotic blistering on the rudder. I request permission from FRCS to lift the boat urgently so I could begin the repair process and leave. Their response was to tell me I must give them 150% of the customs value of the boat as a security deposit before I would be allowed to repair my boat. When I asked how they would decide the customs value of the boat, and offered to pay for a marine survey to do that, they stopped replying to my emails.
