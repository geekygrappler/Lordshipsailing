Découverte de la Martinique

Andy Brown

May 2, 2022 • 7 min read

I've been in Martinique for nearly a month now. I've been improving my French and exploring the island. I had planned to continue further North and reach Guadeloupe. However, I don't want to stop at each island for a couple of days (or even a week), so I can say I've 'been' to every island, without really knowing much about each place. I want to really spend time at each place I visit. This dictates that I spend many lunch times and evenings in little cafes and bars, ordering small beers, day after day, night after night, until the proprietor vaguely recognises me and responds to my confident "Ça va?" with a friendly smile and maybe a few words about the French presidential election result.

At this rate I'll complete a circumnavigation by 2033.

A fellow sailor I had met in 2019 in A Coruña, Spain, sailed down from Antigua to meet up with me and another sailor he'd also met in A Coruña. We had all crossed the Atlantic, myself with 4 on board. Darren, 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁳󠁣󠁴󠁿, had done so in his Trident Warrior solo (💪) and Jack, 🇧🇪, had crossed with a long time friend of his who he'd been sailing with for 30 years. When we met at a small bar by beside La Savane Park, the beers began to flow at a comparable rate to the yachting yarns.

We debriefed our crossings, Jack and I had tales of woe, the solo sailor's only contribution was when, 200NM East of Barbados, he came across a de-masted charter boat drifting. He furnished them with his emergency diesel in jerry cans. The cans were passed by rigging up a couple of lines between the boats at a safe distance and tying the cans to one of the lines, passing it back and forth. The boat was a charter, this means the boat is sailed across by a hired skipper (or the business owner if it's a small business), and packed with paying crew members. The boat will usually then be chartered out in the Caribbean for the season, before crossing back in the other direction, again full of fare paying crew members, before doing the same in the Mediterranean. I imagine it's a difficult business with small margins. A couple of days later Darren came across the boat in the Barbados anchorage, Carlisle, where he had a chat with them and asked the Dutch skipper to return the jerry cans when he got a chance. A couple of days later he woke up to see the boat had left without returning the jerry cans. I guess those margins must be even smaller than I initially thought. Relations between cruisers and charterers can be fractious at times, I wonder why 😑.

Jack had a very serious incident on his crossing, that makes my crossing look like a cake walk. This will get a bit technical. The chain plate on his bow (front of the boat), snapped. This is a nightmare scenario, which could easily lead to losing your mast, something all sailors dread. The chain plate is a large stainless steel piece of metal bolted to the boat which anchors the forestay. The forestay is a wire that runs from the top of the mast to the front of the boat, this essentially is the only thing applying forward pressure on the mast. Without it, the forces of the sail, or simply the backstays (opposite of the forestay), could cause the mast to fall down. Jack and his crew caught a break, the furler, which is a cylindrical box at the base of the forestay, used for rolling the head sail in and out, was caught in between the pull pit guard rail, which meant the forestay remained in tension (see the photo). Jack remained calm and came up with a solution, he lashed the furler to the bow cleat, ensuring the forestay, and mast remained secure. They were even able to continue to use the head sail, and continued cautiously to the Caribbean, arriving safely in Martinique.

The alcohol continued to flow and we moved onto a cool little bar in Fort de France (FdF) called Le Garage. We had moved from beers to ti punch, frozen caipirinhas and cuba libres. When Darren handed me a huge glass of tequila with a red Desparado turned upside down in it, I decided it was a fortuitous time to execute a Polish exit. When I finally made it down to the dock, where I had left my dinghy, I only had one oar left. The motion of the dock had managed to unscrew the cap of the rowlock and the oar floated away. My memory of events ends at that point. I think it's accurate to say I put my life at more risk paddling back to the boat that night, without a life jacket, at that level of inebriation, than I did crossing an ocean. I was lucky on this occasion, although I didn't feel like it when I woke up the next morning.

When I finally left the FdF anchorage, which I'd been staying at for 14 days, I had to spend 30 minutes scrubbing the anchor as I pulled it in, removing as much seaweed and slime as possible. I headed across the bay to a more holiday vibe anchorage, where Darren and Jack were. While at anchor, Darren and I became quite concerned about a neighbouring boat. The dinghy was completely full of water and partially deflated. After waiting a day without any sign of activity we decided to go over and check that there wasn't a dead body on board. The companion way was closed but not locked, going below deck we were assaulted with pungent odours, but it was at least vacant. The fridge was full of rotting food. There was a bag full of rubbish under the nav station. There was mould and damp on all the bedding and the clothes in the cupboards. The dinghy also had a trash bag floating in the water. It looked to us like the owner had left for the day and never returned. We bailed out the dinghy a little bit so the water wasn't overflowing. Having ascertained there was no dead body, and that the boat was not actually sinking, we returned to Darren's boat.

When back on the boat, we started to hypothesis, what could have happened? Heart attack? Sudden emergency back in Europe? We had established the boat was Belgian 🇧🇪, so we started trying to figure out if we could find the owner of a boat in Belgium with the boat name and home port. We also started talking about buying the boat. If it was abandoned, and the owner didn't want to sail it any more, maybe we could buy it for a very reasonable price, clean her up, sail her back to Europe and sell her. A project for fun and profit. I had recently met a Danish sailor aboard a 1975, 52 foot (15.8m) cutter. He told me that he'd bought that boat for $50,000 (very cheap) using the profits from flipping three boats in the Caribbean. He would find boats in marinas, where the owners were reluctantly paying the mooring fees, but not using the boat, or had stopped paying the fees all together. He would make them 'cheeky' offers to take the boat off their hands, and then he'd sail it back and double the money every time selling it in Europe.

By this point Darren and I were quite excited at the prospect of a new boat project. Live aboard cruisers tend to be, or at least Darren and I are, people who like projects. We both had our own boats, which required ongoing maintenance work, and we both had ideas for improvement projects, but having owned them for a number of years we had done most of the major work on them. They were as 'finished' as a boat could ever be. Personally I only want to add a water maker, and install the deck shower I bought in the Canaries, oh, and to add... 🙄, see, basically finished. We had an itch, and that abandoned Benateau Cyclades 43' (Saling La Vagabonds first boat) looked like it could be the perfect scratch.

We went back over, for a more prolonged rustle around to check for clues about the owner and the state of the boat. What condition was the engine in? Was there water in the bilge? Were the batteries running? There was damage on the stern sugar scoop (step at back of the boat), how bad was that damage? While we were there we found the customs entry form for Martinique, with the owners name. The boat had arrived on 26th of March, so less than a month ago. It was a surprise how bad a boat could get in less than a month of being unattended. We also found a stack of brochures for a charter company. We contacted the owner via Whatsapp and he told us that he'd just left for a couple of weeks to go back to Belgium and he'd asked the neighbouring boat to keep an eye on the dinghy. He asked us to empty the dinghy and hoist it onto the foredeck. Which to me, sounds like something he should have done before he left his boat at anchor unattended for multiple weeks and flew to a different continent?!? He told us he'd be back next week, so we said we were sure his dinghy wouldn't sink in the next couple of days. I hope he gives the boat a good clean before the next charter guests arrive. Dreams shattered! 😭 Still the seeds have been planted...

I don't have any drone footage from Martinique. Channeling my wise and cautious Lordship crew member emeritus, Mirko, I actually checked the regulations for drone flight in Martinique. There is an 18km exclusion zone around the airport, which is in the centre of the island. As the island is around 30km across and 60km north to south, this means you either need to be on the south or the north coast to fly a drone. Normally I am quite laissez faire about regulations, but we're back in Europe now, which means the authorities might actually enforce the rules and the fine could be up to €70,000.

I plan to leave Martinique in the next week or so. I will head south, stopping at St. Lucia and Carriacou, before going to Trinidad to lay the boat up for the hurricane season.
